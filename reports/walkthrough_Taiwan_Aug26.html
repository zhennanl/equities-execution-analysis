2Which data the review uses

The screens MSCI runs to decide which companies are eligible for the index, and the data each one is run on.

Where the rules sit in the rulebook. GIMI methodology, May 2026 edition, *§3.1.9 Date of Data Used for Index Reviews, p.48*, which sets three separate cutoffs:

  • Equity Universe Cutoff — last business day of May for an August review. Governs universe construction (§2.1) and the Equity Universe Minimum Size Requirement (§3.1.2.2).
  • Liquidity Cutoff — last business day of June for an August review. Governs ATVR and frequency of trading.
  • Price Cutoff — any one of the last 10 business days of July for an August review. Governs prices for market cap, FIF updates (§3.1.7), foreign room, and NOS (§3.1.8).

    • Investability screens. On top of size and liquidity, MSCI applies a further set of tests that a security has to pass to be added to the index or kept in it:

  • Foreign room (§2.2.8, §2.3.6.2) — room left under a foreign ownership limit must be at least 15% of the maximum allowed.
  • Frequency of trading (§2.2.5) — an existing constituent needs a 3-month ATVR of at least 5% and traded-day frequency of at least 80% in developed markets, 70% in emerging.
  • Minimum length of trading (§2.2.7) — three months of trading before the review is implemented, which is what keeps most IPOs out until the following review.
  • Extreme price increase (§2.3.6.3) — a security whose excess return breaches MSCI's thresholds is barred from ADDITION to the Standard Index and re-tested next review. A name can pass every size and liquidity test and still not be added because it rose too fast.
  • Surveillance boards (Appendix I, Other Cases) — securities that enter the Taiwan Disposition Board, India's ASM lists, Indonesia's Watchlist Board or Korea's Investment Alert and Investment Risk lists get no IMI addition and no Standard-Small migration.

    • 3Where the size cutoff sits

    Every threshold in this step is one link in a single chain. None of them is a number MSCI publishes for Taiwan; each is derived from the one above it.

    Where each number comes from. GIMI methodology, May 2026 edition — the current one; there is no August 2026 edition.

  • Global Minimum Size Reference — §2.3.2.1, p.24-25. The published worked example, priced at the close of 2026-04-20, gives DM Standard $15.75B and EM Standard $7.87B ("set at one-half the corresponding level").
  • Global Minimum Size Range — §2.3.2, p.24: "a range of 0.5 times to 1.15 times those References". The book prints the answer: EM Standard $3.94B to $9.06B.
  • Market Size-Segment Cutoff — §2.3.3, p.26. The 85% walk over the market's own investable universe, bounded into the range above.
  • Buffer zones — §3.1.5.1, p.44: "the buffer zones at Index Reviews are defined with boundaries of 2/3rd of and 1.5 times the Market Size-Segment Cutoff".
  • Minimum free float — §2.3.6.1, p.30: a new Standard constituent needs free-float-adjusted cap of at least 50% of the cutoff, here $3.37B.

    • Two figures here are ours, not MSCI's. The reference is shown as $16.41B rather than the published $15.75B because the published value is priced at 2026-04-20 and this review prices in late July; §3.1.3 confirms the references are reset at every review, so scaling is the right shape of adjustment, but the +4.2% scalar is our proxy and carries a declared ±2pt band. Taiwan's crossing is likewise computed from our float stack, not from MSCI's.

    A correction, on the record.     Every company gets two numbers at the cutoff date, and they do two different jobs. Full market capitalisation — that day's close times shares issued, in USD — is the sort key. Free-float-adjusted capitalisation is the running total. Confusing the two is the classic way to get this walk wrong.

    Two definitions worth getting exactly right. The FIF is "the proportion of shares outstanding that is available for purchase in the public equity markets by international investors" (§2.2.6) — it accounts for free float and for foreign ownership limits, so it is not simply free float. And footnote 5 on p.17 states that MSCI's free-float-adjusted cap is measured after any relevant adjustment factors, including low-foreign-room adjustments and Liquidity Adjustment Factors. We approximate it as full cap times FIF, which is right for almost every name and wrong for any name carrying an adjustment factor we cannot see.

    One refinement matters. MSCI does not count shares that will never trade — a government stake, a founding family block. Most outcomes are decided on full cap, and we flag the names where float is doing the work, because float is the input we are least sure of.

  • Sort by FULL cap — size-segment membership is a statement about how big a company is, and full cap is the market's own view of that. Float is an accessibility adjustment, not a size measure. Sorting on float would let a widely held mid-cap outrank a larger, tightly held company, and the index would stop being a size index.
  • Cumulate FLOAT cap — the 85% coverage target (§2.3.1) is a promise about what an investor can actually buy. Measured on full cap it would count shares no foreign investor can purchase, and the index would be claiming coverage it cannot deliver.
  • Read the crossing company's FULL cap — the output is a size threshold applied to companies, so it has to come back in the same units as the sort key.
  • Company level for size, security level for float — §2.2.3 aggregates a company's securities because size-segments are assigned to companies (§2.3.3: "all securities of a company are always classified in the same size-segment"). §2.2.4's float test is applied per security, because tradability belongs to a listing, not to a company.
  • The 0.15 FIF floor (§2.2.6) and the separate 50%-of-minimum-size float test (§2.2.4) exist because a large company with a tiny float is not investable at size — the company-level size screen alone would let it through.

    • The float stack, best source first. (1) FIFs implied by MSCI's own factsheet, top-10, exact same date. (2) MSCI's own member FIFs recovered by inverting the published index weights — these land on MSCI's 2.5% rounding grid, which is how we know the inversion is right. (3) Yahoo floatShares/sharesOutstanding, 2.7% median error against MSCI on the aligned overlap and the best public source. (4) TDCC bracket float scaled by a calibration measured on the Yahoo overlap, since TDCC treats large domestic institutions as strategic where MSCI treats them as float. At 2026-07-31 the mix ran 10 / 53 / ~490 / ~1,460 names, with nothing falling through to a bare default. Any name whose verdict flips between adjacent tiers is marked borderline and not called.

    The arithmetic, in full. Both lines are the step-3 cutoff multiplied by a constant — there is nothing else in them.

    ``` Market Size-Segment Cutoff $6.74B (step 3) Lower buffer = 2/3 x cutoff = $4.49B Upper buffer = 1.5 x cutoff = $10.11B Min float cap = 0.5 x cutoff = $3.37B ```

    The two sides are not symmetric, and that is the part worth carrying. A deletion is ONE test: an existing constituent is held while its full cap stays at or above the lower buffer, and migrates down below it. An addition is FOUR, all of which must pass together — full cap at or above the upper buffer (§3.1.5.1), free-float-adjusted cap at or above half the cutoff (§2.3.6.1), an FIF of at least 0.15 (§2.2.6), and foreign room of at least 15% (§2.2.8).

    So the addition side is the harder call, and it fails for reasons that have nothing to do with size. A name can clear the bar on cap and still not join because its float is too thin — which is exactly what happens to one of the candidates below.

    A candidate is also not a certainty in the other direction: MSCI sweeps a subset of the below-floor pool rather than all of it, and some below-floor names survive for years.

    Where the conviction number comes from. Each call carries a percentage, and it is built by one multiplication chain with no model behind it:

    ``` probability = base rate for the name's ZONE x one haircut per declared weakness ```

    The zone is which side of which line the name sits on — above the 1.5x bar, in the 1.0-1.5x queue, below the floor, failing the float gate. The haircuts are the things we know are wrong with our own inputs: the member count can flex under us (x0.85, §2.3.3), the float is our estimate rather than MSCI's (x0.90), liquidity was not evaluated for that name (x0.85), and for additions only, names we cannot see may take the slots (x0.95).

    Two things follow, and both matter more than the number itself. Most of the distance from the base rate to the final figure is uncertainty about OUR data, not about MSCI — an addition starting at 85% arrives at 62% without MSCI doing anything. And the base rates are registered judgements, declared in advance and deliberately coarse — five buckets rather than a false-precision continuum — not frequencies fitted to history.

    They are uncalibrated. Nobody has yet checked whether names called at 62% happen 62% of the time, and with 16 calls on one review nobody can. Calibration needs the grades from several reviews, which is the argument for declaring in advance every quarter rather than once. Treat the percentage as a RANKING today and as a probability only after it has been scored.

    7Where This Analysis Stops Working

    Five limits. A method is only tradeable if you know where it breaks.

    Float is the weak input, and it decides the borderline cases. Free float is estimated, not published. Ours is current-vintage rather than point-in-time — fine for full-cap tests, not fine for the float gate, so we skip that gate historically rather than fake it.

    Discretion cannot be modelled. §3.1.9 alone lets MSCI decline a migration on a takeover bid or a suspension, and no amount of data predicts a judgement call. MSCI can also switch a review to a light rebalancing, which widens the buffers from 2/3 and 1.5x the cutoff to 0.5x and 1.8x — here $3.37B and $12.13B, enough to take two names off the addition list. It is a market-stress provision, not a cadence one: p.107 sets a monitoring period over the last ten business days before the announcement, and a breach refers the decision to MSCI's Equity Index Committee and Index Policy Committee. No switch has been declared for this review, so 2/3 and 1.5 stand — but it is the one rule change that arrives with almost no notice, so it is worth watching in those ten days.

    Blind spots. A name that has never entered the measured universe is invisible to us, so a large new listing can be added with no warning. Names also leave between reviews — takeovers, delistings, sanctions — and those never appear in a review list at all. And surveillance boards are the one screen in step 2 we do not harvest at all: the Taiwan Disposition Board and its peers would have to be tracked continuously rather than snapshotted at a cutoff, so a name barred by one looks qualified to us right up until it is not added.

    Outside Taiwan there is no track record yet. Taiwan and India price from archival exchange day-files, which still contain delisted companies. Every other APAC market prices from Yahoo, which carries live listings only — fine for the next review, useless for backtesting past ones, because the names MSCI deleted are exactly the ones Yahoo has dropped. The forward call is real; the hit rate is not measured.

    The conviction numbers are uncalibrated. See step 6: registered judgements, never scored.
